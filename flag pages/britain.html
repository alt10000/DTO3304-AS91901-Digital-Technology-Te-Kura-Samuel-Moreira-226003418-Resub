Historical Flags

British Flags

Flag of the Kingdom of Great Britain (1707–1800)

Following the Acts of Union in 1707, which united the Kingdoms of England and Scotland, the existing Union Flag of 1606 became the official flag of Great Britain. It featured the red Cross of St George, representing England and Wales, superimposed on the white Saltire of St Andrew, representing Scotland, against a blue background.

Flag of the United Kingdom (1801–present)

Introduced on 1 January 1801 following the union of Great Britain and Ireland, the new flag incorporated the red Saltire of St Patrick into the existing Union Flag. The resulting design, commonly known as the Union Jack or Union Flag, combines the crosses of St George, St Andrew and St Patrick to represent England, Scotland and Ireland (now Northern Ireland).

Historical note: The Union Flag originated in 1606, following the Union of the Crowns under James VI of Scotland and I of England, but only became the official flag of the united Kingdom of Great Britain in 1707. The modern design has remained unchanged since 1801, including after most of Ireland became independent in 1922.

English Flags

Flag of England – St George's Cross (c. 1277–present)

The red cross of St George on a white field became associated with English soldiers during the reign of King Edward I in the late 13th century. It gradually developed into England's national flag and became particularly prominent under Edward III, who established St George as England's principal patron saint in 1348.

Banner of St Edward (1042–1066)

Used as the royal banner of Edward the Confessor, this flag featured a golden cross with fleur-de-lis-shaped ends on a blue field, surrounded by five golden birds known as martlets. It represented the royal authority of one of the last Anglo-Saxon kings of England and remained an important part of English heraldic tradition.

Fighting Banner of Harold Godwinson (1066)

Associated with King Harold II during the Norman invasion of England, this banner reportedly depicted a fighting warrior embroidered in gold. It was carried into the Battle of Hastings in 1066, where Harold's forces were defeated by William the Conqueror; the banner's exact appearance is uncertain and its modern depiction is a reconstruction based on medieval accounts.

Royal Banner of England (c. 1198–present)

The royal banner of England features three golden lions passant guardant, arranged vertically on a red field. The design originated with the heraldic arms of Richard I and became the enduring symbol of the English monarchy, later forming part of the Royal Standard of the United Kingdom.

Royal Banner of King Richard I (1198–1340)

Introduced during the reign of Richard I, also known as Richard the Lionheart, this banner featured three golden lions on a red background. The design developed from the royal heraldic arms associated with Richard and became the foundation of the royal arms of England.

Royal Banner of King Edward III (1340–1395; 1399–1406)

Edward III introduced a quartered royal banner combining England's three golden lions with the golden fleurs-de-lis of France on a blue background. The French symbols represented his claim to the French throne, which became a major justification for the Hundred Years' War.

Royal Banner of King Richard II (1395–1399)

This banner combined the three golden lions of England with the attributed coat of arms of Edward the Confessor, featuring a golden cross and martlets on a blue field. The combination symbolised Richard II's devotion to Edward the Confessor and his attempt to associate his monarchy with the revered Anglo-Saxon king.

Royal Banner of King Henry IV (1406–1422)

Henry IV modified the royal banner by replacing the numerous fleurs-de-lis representing France with three fleurs-de-lis, arranged to simplify the French quartering. The banner retained the three golden lions of England and continued to represent the English monarchy for several subsequent reigns.

Royal Banner of Queen Mary I and King Philip (1554–1558)

Following the marriage of Mary I of England to Philip II of Spain in 1554, a joint royal banner was introduced, combining the royal arms of England with those of the Habsburg monarchy. The design represented the political and dynastic union between the English and Spanish crowns during their brief joint reign.

Royal Standard of the House of Stuart (1603–1649; 1660–1689; 1702–1707)

First introduced by James VI of Scotland and I of England following the Union of the Crowns in 1603, this banner featured the quartered royal arms of England, Scotland and Ireland, alongside the French fleurs-de-lis representing England's historical claim to the French throne. It was the final royal banner used by the independent Kingdom of England before the Acts of Union in 1707.

Royal Standard of King William III and II (1694–1702)

Following the death of Queen Mary II in 1694, William III continued to use a modified royal standard featuring the combined arms of England, Scotland and Ireland, alongside the French fleurs-de-lis. A small central shield bearing the orange lion of the House of Nassau was retained to represent William's Dutch ancestry and dynastic heritage.

White Dragon Flag of England (traditional Anglo-Saxon symbolism)

The white dragon is traditionally associated with the Anglo-Saxons through the medieval Welsh legend of the red and white dragons, in which the white dragon represents the Saxons and the red dragon represents the Britons. Although the white dragon became an important historical and mythical symbol, there is no reliable evidence that it was ever used as an official English national flag during the Anglo-Saxon period; modern flag depictions are largely reconstructions.

Historical note: English flags evolved from medieval religious banners and royal heraldry into national symbols. The St George's Cross gradually became England's national flag, while the royal banners changed according to the monarch's dynastic claims, marriages and political ambitions. The white dragon, unlike the other examples, is primarily a legendary symbol rather than a historically documented national flag.

Scottish Flags

Royal Banner of Scotland (c. 1222–present)

First recorded during the reign of King Alexander II, this banner features a red lion rampant with blue claws and tongue, surrounded by a red double border decorated with fleurs-de-lis on a golden-yellow field. It became the royal standard of the Scottish monarchy and remains an important symbol of Scotland's royal heritage, although its official use is now restricted to the Sovereign and authorised representatives.

Scottish Union Flag (1606–1707)

Introduced following the Union of the Crowns in 1603 under James VI of Scotland and I of England, this flag combined the white Saltire of St Andrew on a blue background with the red Cross of St George on a white background. Unlike the English version of the Union Flag, the Scottish design placed St Andrew's Cross above St George's Cross, reflecting Scottish objections to the English cross taking precedence.

Flag of Scotland – St Andrew's Cross (c. 1542–present)

Also known as the Saltire, Scotland's national flag features a white diagonal cross on a blue field, representing St Andrew, the country's patron saint. Although traditionally associated with the legendary Battle of Athelstaneford in 832, its earliest confirmed illustration in this familiar design dates to approximately 1542, and it remains Scotland's national flag today.

Historical note: Scotland's flags have two particularly important traditions: the Royal Banner, representing the Scottish monarchy, and the Saltire, representing the Scottish nation. The Scottish Union Flag was a transitional design following the Union of the Crowns in 1603 and preceded the adoption of the modern Union Flag in 1707.
