Slavic Peoples

About the Slavic group

The Slavic peoples are the largest ethnolinguistic group in Europe. They are traditionally divided into West Slavs (Poles, Czechs, Slovaks), East Slavs (Russians, Ukrainians, Belarusians) and South Slavs (Slovenes, Croats, Serbs, Bosniaks, Montenegrins, Macedonians, Bulgarians).

Their flags show how a shared sense of kinship was sometimes expressed through common colours, and how national movements later pulled in different directions.

Flags side by side

The Pan-Slavic colours

At the Slavic Congress held in Prague in 1848, white, blue and red were taken up as colours of Slavic unity. They came from the Russian flag, since Russia was the largest independent Slavic state at the time. Many Slavic countries later used the colours when they won their own flags.

Variations on a theme

Not every Slavic flag followed the pattern exactly. Bulgaria swapped blue for green, while Poland and Czechia built their flags from the white and red of their own heraldry. The result is a family of flags that look related without being copies.

The double-headed eagle

Russia, Serbia and Montenegro all carried a double-headed eagle on historic flags. The symbol spread through the Orthodox world from the Byzantine imperial tradition, and each of these states made it part of its own royal or imperial identity.

The red star years

After the Second World War, Slavic republics inside Yugoslavia and the Soviet bloc kept their traditional colours but added a red star. The star marked a shared communist identity, and it disappeared again from these flags around 1990.

Where they differ

Ukraine, Belarus and North Macedonia chose colours that have nothing to do with the Pan-Slavic tricolour. Their flags reflect local history, and show that language kinship never produced a single Slavic flag tradition.
