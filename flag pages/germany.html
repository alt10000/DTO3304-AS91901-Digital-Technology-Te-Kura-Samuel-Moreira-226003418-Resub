Germany

Historical Flags

War Flag of the Holy Roman Empire (12th century)

The early imperial war banner is associated with a red field bearing a white cross, used as a military standard of the Holy Roman Empire. Medieval imperial banners changed over time, and surviving depictions do not always agree on the precise design.

Imperial Pavilion of the Holy Roman Empire (1400–1523)

This imperial standard featured a black double-headed eagle on a golden-yellow field. The double-headed eagle represented the authority of the Holy Roman Emperor and became one of the most recognisable symbols of the Empire.

Flag of the Teutonic Order State (1226–1525)

The Teutonic Knights began as a crusader hospital order at Acre in 1190 and built a state in Prussia and the Baltic from 1226, after being invited to fight the pagan Prussians. Their black cross on white echoed the black cross on the knights’ white mantles. The state ended in 1525 when Grand Master Albert of Brandenburg turned it into the secular Duchy of Prussia.

Flag of Royal Prussia (1466–1772)

This flag displayed a black eagle on a white field, with a golden crown around its neck and an armoured arm holding a sword emerging from it. It represented Royal Prussia, an autonomous region under the Polish Crown following the Second Peace of Thorn in 1466.

Second Flag of the Kingdom in Prussia and First Flag of the Kingdom of Prussia (1750–1801)

This design featured a crowned black Prussian eagle on a white field, holding a sword and sceptre. The eagle symbolised the authority of the Hohenzollern dynasty, while the letters FR on its breast stood for Fredericus Rex (King Frederick).

Second Flag of the Kingdom of Prussia (1801–1803)

Under Frederick William III the royal standard kept the crowned black eagle on white, but the eagle was redrawn in the style of the new reign, holding its sword and sceptre. The standard served a kingdom that had grown through the partitions of Poland and would soon be shaken by defeat against Napoleon in 1806.

Third Flag of the Kingdom of Prussia (1803–1892)

This flag with the crowned black eagle on white flew over Prussia through its defeat by Napoleon in 1806, the Wars of Liberation, and Bismarck’s wars that created the German Empire in 1871. The eagle was the emblem of the Hohenzollern kings, and black and white were the colours that later helped form the imperial flag.

Fourth Flag of the Kingdom of Prussia (1892–1918)

In 1892 Prussia adopted a white flag with black stripes at the top and bottom and a crowned eagle in the centre, fixing the black-and-white colours it had long used. It flew within the German Empire until Wilhelm II abdicated in 1918, ending the Prussian monarchy.

Flag of the Free State of Prussia (1918–1933)

After the monarchy fell in 1918 the Free State of Prussia kept its black and white and used a black-white-black flag bearing a new, uncrowned eagle. It was a republican flag within the Weimar Republic and lasted until the Nazi takeover in 1933 ended Prussia’s independence.

Flag of the German Confederation (1848–1849)

The black-red-gold horizontal tricolour was adopted on 9 March 1848 during the revolutionary movement for German unity. It became a symbol of national unity, constitutional government and civil liberties, and was also used by the short-lived Frankfurt Parliament's proposed German Empire.

Flag of the German Empire (1871–1918)

The black-white-red horizontal tricolour became the national flag of the newly unified German Empire in 1871 and was officially designated in 1892. Its colours combined the traditional black and white of Prussia with the red associated with the Hanseatic cities and Brandenburg.

National Flag of Germany (1919–1933)

The Weimar Republic adopted the black-red-gold horizontal tricolour in 1919, reviving the colours of the democratic revolutions of 1848. It replaced the imperial black-white-red flag and became a symbol of Germany's new republican government.

National Flag of Germany (1933–1935)

After the Nazi seizure of power, the black-white-red imperial tricolour was reintroduced alongside the Nazi Party flag, which featured a red field, a white disc and a black swastika. Both designs were officially used as national flags during this transitional period, until the Nazi flag became the sole national flag in September 1935.

National Flag of Germany (1935–1945)

On 15 September 1935, the Nazi Party flag became Germany's sole national flag. It featured a red field with a white disc and a black swastika positioned slightly off-centre; it remained in use until the collapse of Nazi Germany in 1945.

C-Pennant (C-Doppelstander) (1946–1949)

Following the Allied occupation of Germany, German merchant ships were prohibited from using a national flag. Instead, they were required to fly the international signal flag C, a blue-white-red horizontally striped double-tailed pennant, to identify their German registration.

East German State Flag (Staatsflagge) (1959–1990)

Adopted on 1 October 1959, this flag consisted of the traditional black-red-gold horizontal tricolour with the national emblem of East Germany in the centre. The emblem featured a hammer and compass surrounded by a wreath of grain, symbolising workers, intellectuals and farmers; it was discontinued upon German reunification in 1990.

National Flag of Germany (1949–present)

The modern German flag consists of three horizontal stripes of black, red and gold. Adopted by West Germany in 1949 and retained following reunification on 3 October 1990, it revived the democratic colours associated with the German national movement of the 19th century.
