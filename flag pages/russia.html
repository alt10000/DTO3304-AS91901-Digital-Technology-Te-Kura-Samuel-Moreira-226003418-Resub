Historical Flags

Russian flag history includes several distinct traditions: the early naval ensigns of the Tsardom, the white-blue-red merchant tricolour associated with Peter the Great, the black-yellow-white imperial state flag, and the red banners of the Soviet period. One important distinction is that the tricolour was used as a civil and merchant ensign long before it became Russia's universally recognised national flag.

The list below separates the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic (RSFSR) flags from the flags of the Soviet Union itself. After the USSR was formed in 1922, the RSFSR flag represented one constituent republic, not the entire Union.

Civil ensign of the Tsardom of Russia (1668–1693)

Associated with the Oryol, the first Russian-built warship, constructed during the reign of Tsar Alexis I. In 1668, white, blue and red were selected as the ship's colours, and a tricolour is traditionally reconstructed from this evidence. However, the precise arrangement of the colours is unknown; the familiar horizontal white-blue-red version is only one possible reconstruction. It is also uncertain whether the flag carried a double-headed eagle.

Civil ensign of the Tsardom of Russia (1693–1721)

On 6 August 1693, Peter the Great raised the so-called Flag of the Tsar of Muscovy aboard his yacht Saint Peter. It consisted of three horizontal stripes of white, blue and red, with a golden double-headed eagle in the centre. The surviving original is preserved in the Central Naval Museum in Saint Petersburg. This flag was a royal and maritime ensign, rather than yet being a universally established national flag.

Civil ensign of the Russian Empire (1721–1896)

The plain white-blue-red horizontal tricolour became the principal merchant ensign under Peter the Great, who formally prescribed it for Russian merchant vessels in 1705. It continued to be used after the proclamation of the Russian Empire in 1721. The three colours appeared in equal horizontal bands, with white at the top, blue in the middle and red at the bottom. During much of the nineteenth century, it remained the civil and merchant flag even while a different design served as the imperial state flag.

State flag of the Russian Empire (1858–1896)

Introduced by Emperor Alexander II on 11 June 1858, this flag consisted of three horizontal stripes of black, yellow and white. Its colours were derived from the heraldic colours of the Russian imperial coat of arms: the black double-headed eagle, the golden field and the white figure of Saint George. It was intended primarily for official state use and government buildings, while the white-blue-red tricolour continued as the merchant ensign.

State flag of the Russian Empire (1896–1917)

Under Nicholas II, the white-blue-red tricolour was confirmed as Russia's national flag in 1896, replacing the black-yellow-white design for official national use. Although Alexander III had authorised the tricolour for decorating buildings on public occasions in 1883, the 1896 decision established it as the recognised national flag in all circumstances. It retained the familiar three horizontal stripes, with white above blue and red.

National flag of the Russian Republic (1917–1918)

Following the February Revolution of 1917 and the abdication of Nicholas II, the Provisional Government retained the white-blue-red tricolour. The flag continued to represent Russia after the monarchy was abolished, as it was already widely recognised as the country's national flag. The design remained plain, without the imperial coat of arms or any other emblem.

National flag of the Russian State (1918–1920)

During the Russian Civil War, anti-Bolshevik authorities associated with the Russian State continued to use the white-blue-red tricolour. The government under Admiral Alexander Kolchak, established in Omsk in 1918, did not introduce a completely new national flag. The tricolour therefore remained a symbol of the anti-Bolshevik state and White movement, in contrast with the red flags of the Bolsheviks.

State flag of the RSFSR (April–June 1918)

On 13 April 1918, the All-Russian Central Executive Committee prescribed a red banner bearing the full name of the new republic in Russian: Российская Социалистическая Федеративная Советская Республика (Russian Socialist Federative Soviet Republic). However, the decree did not specify the lettering's size, position or colour, nor the flag's proportions. No reliable evidence confirms that a flag following this exact description was actually manufactured and used. It is therefore generally treated as a proposed or theoretical design.

State flag of the RSFSR (1918–1937)

On 17 June 1918, a more specific design was approved. It consisted of a red field with the golden Cyrillic abbreviation Р.С.Ф.С.Р. in the upper-left corner, written in an ornamental Old Church Slavonic-inspired style and enclosed by a thin golden border. The flag had a width-to-length ratio of 1:2. Later constitutional revisions altered the prescribed lettering, but the original 1918 design continued in use until 1937.

State flag of the RSFSR (1937–1954)

The red field was retained, but the abbreviation in the upper-left corner was changed to the more modern Cyrillic form РСФСР, without the full stops. The lettering remained golden and was placed within a decorative rectangular border. The design was established under the 1937 Constitution and continued until the adoption of a new flag in 1954.

State flag of the RSFSR (1954–1991)

Adopted on 9 January 1954, this flag was based on the Soviet Union's red flag but featured a broad light-blue vertical stripe along the hoist, representing the republic's distinctive identity within the USSR. A golden hammer and sickle and a red five-pointed star outlined in gold appeared in the upper-left portion of the red field. It remained the RSFSR's state flag until the restoration of the white-blue-red tricolour in August 1991.

State flag of the RSFSR (1991–1993)

Following the failed August Coup in 1991, the white-blue-red tricolour was restored as the republic's state flag on 22 August. It was formally adopted on 1 November 1991, replacing the Soviet-style flag with the light-blue hoist stripe. After the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991, the same design continued as the flag of the Russian Federation. It remained in use until the adoption of the modern flag legislation in 1993.

State flag of the Soviet Union (1922–1923)

Following the establishment of the USSR on 30 December 1922, an initial design was proposed featuring a red field with the Soviet state coat of arms in the centre, outlined in white. The emblem represented the union of the Soviet republics through its hammer and sickle, globe, wheat wreath and red star. This early design was short-lived and apparently never widely manufactured or used.

State flag of the Soviet Union (1923–1924)

A revised design replaced the large central coat of arms with a golden hammer and sickle and a red five-pointed star outlined in gold, positioned in the upper-left corner. The symbols were enclosed within a golden-bordered canton. This was an intermediate design during the development of the familiar Soviet flag, before the final layout was constitutionally specified in 1924.

State flag of the Soviet Union (1924–1936)

The 1924 Constitution established the familiar red flag with a golden hammer and sickle in the upper-left corner and a red five-pointed star outlined in gold above them. The flag's proportions were set at 1:2. The design symbolised the political alliance between industrial workers and peasants, while the red star represented communist ideology and the international socialist movement.

State flag of the Soviet Union (1936–1955)

The 1936 Constitution retained the hammer-and-sickle and star design, but the legal description of the flag was updated alongside the adoption of the new Soviet Constitution. The red field and golden symbols remained unchanged in their basic appearance. This was the version prominently used throughout the Second World War, including during the Soviet capture of Berlin in 1945.

State flag of the Soviet Union (1955–1991)

On 19 August 1955, the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet adopted a regulation standardising the flag's proportions and the exact positioning and dimensions of its symbols. Its appearance remained essentially the same as the 1936 version: a red field with a golden hammer and sickle and a red, gold-bordered star in the upper-left corner. It remained the Soviet Union's state flag until the country's dissolution in December 1991.

Civil and state flag of the Russian Federation (1993–present)

The white-blue-red horizontal tricolour was formally re-established as Russia's national flag after the dissolution of the Soviet Union. A presidential decree in 1993 confirmed the familiar arrangement, and the Federal Constitutional Law of 25 December 2000 established its legal description: three equal horizontal stripes of white, blue and red, with a width-to-length ratio of 2:3. Unlike the imperial and Soviet state flags, the modern design contains no coat of arms or other emblem.

Historical note

The white-blue-red tricolour originated in Russia's maritime history during the seventeenth century. It became the merchant ensign under Peter the Great in 1705 and eventually the national flag of the Russian Empire in 1896. After the Soviet period, it was restored in 1991 and remains the flag of the Russian Federation.

Historical clarification

  • The 1668 flag is not known with certainty. The colours white, blue and red are documented, but the exact arrangement and whether it displayed a double-headed eagle remain subjects of historical discussion.
  • The 1693 flag is a surviving historical object. Unlike the uncertain 1668 reconstruction, the white-blue-red flag with the golden double-headed eagle is preserved in a museum.
  • The 1858–1896 black-yellow-white flag and the white-blue-red tricolour served different roles. The former was the imperial state flag, while the latter continued as the civil and merchant ensign for much of that period.
  • The Russian Republic and Russian State did not introduce distinct national flag designs. Both continued using the white-blue-red tricolour.
  • The April–June 1918 RSFSR design was not securely documented as an actual manufactured flag. The June 1918 version was the first with an officially approved illustration.
  • The 1922–1924 Soviet Union flags were transitional designs. The familiar hammer-and-sickle flag was established in November 1923 and constitutionally specified in 1924.
  • The 1936 and 1955 Soviet flags looked almost identical to the preceding version. The 1955 regulation chiefly standardised the design's proportions and details.
  • The current Russian flag is the same basic white-blue-red tricolour used under the Russian Empire, although its modern legal status and specifications were established after the Soviet Union's collapse.
