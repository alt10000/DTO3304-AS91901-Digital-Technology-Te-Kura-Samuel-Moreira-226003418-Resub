Likely royal banner of Norway (13th–14th centuries)

This medieval banner is generally reconstructed as a red field bearing a golden lion rampant holding a silver axe. The lion derives from the Norwegian royal coat of arms and became the principal symbol of the Norwegian monarchy. One of the earliest known depictions of this banner appears on the seal of Duchess Ingebjørg Håkonsdatter from 1318. It is regarded as the predecessor of Norway's modern royal standard.

Dannebrogen (c. 16th century–1814)

During the union with Denmark, Norway used the Danish Dannebrog: a red field with a white Scandinavian cross extending to the edges. It became increasingly common on Norwegian ships, fortresses and military establishments, and from 1748 was the only officially recognised merchant flag of the Danish-Norwegian state. The flag's legendary origin dates to the Battle of Lyndanisse in 1219, when it supposedly fell from heaven, although this story is not supported by contemporary evidence.

Flag of Denmark–Norway (1537–1814)

The composite Danish-Norwegian monarchy generally used the same red flag with a white Nordic cross. Merchant vessels flew the rectangular Dannebrog, while naval and royal vessels commonly used a swallow-tailed version. The flag represented the shared monarchy rather than Norway alone, as the two kingdoms were governed by the same monarch and increasingly integrated politically.

Flag of Norway (1814–1821)

Following the dissolution of the union with Denmark, Regent Christian Frederik proclaimed a Norwegian flag on 27 February 1814. It retained the red field and white cross of the Dannebrog but added the Norwegian golden lion with its silver axe in the upper-left canton. The design served as Norway's state and naval flag during its brief period of independence and continued as a merchant flag until 1821.

State flag of Norway and Sweden (1815–1844)

Following the establishment of the union between Norway and Sweden, a common naval and state flag was introduced on 7 March 1815. It was based on the Swedish swallow-tailed flag, with a red canton containing a white diagonal cross (saltire), representing Norway's former connection with Denmark. The design served as the common military ensign and state flag of both kingdoms.

Union mark of Norway (1844–1905)

Known informally as the Sildesalaten (“herring salad”), the union mark combined the colours of the Norwegian and Swedish flags in a quartered pattern of red, white, blue and yellow. Introduced by King Oscar I on 20 June 1844, it was placed in the upper-left canton of the national, state and naval flags of both kingdoms to symbolise their union and equal status. Norway removed it from its merchant and state flags in 1899, although it remained on the naval ensign until the dissolution of the union in 1905.

Flag of the Reichskommissariat Norwegen (1940–1945)

During the German occupation of Norway in the Second World War, the Reichskommissariat Norwegen used the national flag of Nazi Germany: a red field with a white disc containing a black swastika. The flag represented the German occupation administration headed by Reichskommissar Josef Terboven. Norwegian national symbols were suppressed, and the traditional Norwegian flag became an important symbol of resistance and national identity.

National flag of Norway (1821–present; fully restored in 1899 and 1905)

Designed by Member of Parliament Frederik Meltzer, the modern Norwegian flag was adopted by the Storting in May 1821. It consists of a red field with a blue Scandinavian cross outlined in white, extending to the edges. The design combines the traditional Nordic cross pattern with red, white and blue colours, which were associated with liberty and constitutional government. The union emblem was removed from the merchant and state flags in 1899 and from the naval ensign following the dissolution of the union with Sweden in 1905.

Historical note: Norway's flag history reflects its changing political relationships with Denmark and Sweden. The medieval royal banner featured the golden Norwegian lion on red, while the Dannebrog represented Norway during its union with Denmark. The red-white-blue cross flag emerged in 1821 and eventually became the symbol of an independent Norwegian nation.

Historical clarification

  • The medieval royal banner is a reconstruction based on heraldic evidence, rather than a surviving standardised national flag.
  • The Dannebrog was the common flag of Denmark–Norway, not an exclusively Norwegian flag.
  • The 1815 union flag was primarily a state and naval ensign; Norway's merchant ships used different arrangements during the early years of the Swedish-Norwegian union.
  • The 1844 union mark was not a separate national flag, but an emblem inserted into the canton of the two kingdoms' flags.
  • Norway's modern tricolour was adopted in 1821, but the union mark was not completely removed from all Norwegian flags until 1905.
