Portugal

Historical Flags

Portugal's early royal flags evolved alongside the country's coat of arms. The successive designs generally altered the number and arrangement of the blue escutcheons and the red bordure, while retaining the white shield and blue-and-white heraldic tradition. These were primarily royal and military standards; Portugal did not have a modern national flag in the contemporary sense during the medieval period.

A useful distinction for this sequence: some dates mark a change in the monarch, not necessarily a newly adopted flag. For example, the 1521 entry largely continues the design established under Manuel I, while the 1826 entry belongs to the final period of the white royal flag. The Parques de Sintra historical timeline is particularly helpful for following these successive royal arms.

Royal flag (1095–1143)

Traditionally reconstructed as a blue cross on a white or silver field, associated with Henry of Burgundy, Count of Portugal, and the early County of Portugal. This design is hypothetical: there is no secure contemporary evidence that it was used as a flag in this period. It was proposed in later heraldic interpretations of Portugal's origins.

Royal flag (1143–1185)

Associated with Afonso I (Afonso Henriques), the first King of Portugal. The traditional reconstruction shows a blue cross on a white field, with five smaller blue shields arranged over it and bearing silver bezants (small round discs). The design is also uncertain, and the idea that the five shields developed from a damaged cross on Afonso's personal shield is a later theory rather than an established fact.

Royal flag (1185–1248)

Under Sancho I and his successors, the cross was replaced by five blue escutcheons arranged in the form of a cross on a white field. Each shield carried a number of silver bezants, which eventually became fixed at five per shield. This is the earliest form of the Portuguese royal arms that is generally regarded as historically established.

Royal flag (1248–1385)

Afonso III introduced a red border around the five blue escutcheons, decorated with golden castles. The change is traditionally associated with his dynastic connections to Castile, through his marriage to Beatrice of Castile. The number and arrangement of castles were not yet completely standardised.

Royal flag (1385–1485)

With the accession of John I and the establishment of the House of Aviz, the royal arms were modified to include a green cross of the Order of Aviz over the red border and golden castles. The five blue escutcheons and their bezants remained central elements. The design reflected the new dynasty's association with the military order.

Royal flag (1485–1521)

King John II fixed the royal arms as five blue shields, each with five silver coins, set inside a red border of golden castles. Traditionally seven castles became the standard. His reign was a time of expansion, with Portuguese ships exploring the African coast.

Royal flag (1521–1578)

Under John III, the traditional square banner of arms gave way to a rectangular white flag displaying the royal coat of arms in the centre. The shield had a rounded lower edge and was topped by an open royal crown. The design retained the five blue escutcheons and red border with golden castles, but no longer included the green cross of the Order of Aviz.

Royal flag (1578–1640)

King Sebastian was killed at the Battle of Alcácer Quibir in Morocco in 1578 without an heir. After the brief rule of Cardinal Henry, Philip II of Spain took the throne in 1580. Portugal kept its own royal arms and flag under the Spanish Habsburgs, now with a closed crown.

Royal flag (1640–1667)

Following the Portuguese Restoration of Independence in 1640, John IV retained the white flag with the royal coat of arms, but the shield's shape was modified and the closed crown became an important symbol of the restored monarchy. The design represented the return of an independent Portuguese royal dynasty, the House of Braganza.

Royal flag (1667–1706)

Spain finally recognised Portuguese independence in the Treaty of Lisbon in 1668, in the years when Peter II was regent and then king. The royal arms on white were redrawn with a new crown, and some accounts say this version flew especially at royal fortresses and aboard Crown warships.

Royal flag (1706–1816)

Under John V, wealth from Brazilian gold funded a lavish court, and the royal arms were redrawn in the ornate shield shape of the age. The white flag with this shield flew through the 1755 Lisbon earthquake and the Napoleonic invasions, increasingly standing for Portugal as a whole.

Royal flag (1816–1826)

Under John VI, the royal arms were placed over a golden armillary sphere, which represented the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves, established in 1815. The flag retained its white background and crowned Portuguese shield. The armillary sphere was a particularly important symbol of Portugal's maritime and imperial history.

Royal flag (1826–1834)

After John VI died in 1826, Maria II and her uncle Miguel fought over the throne, and Miguel’s seizure of power in 1828 began the Liberal Wars. The white royal flag, now without the armillary sphere, flew for Miguel’s absolutists, while the constitutional liberals used a blue-and-white flag.

Royal flag (1834–1910)

Following the victory of the constitutional liberals in 1834, the blue-and-white flag became the national flag of the constitutional monarchy. It was vertically divided into blue at the hoist and white at the fly, with the royal coat of arms positioned across the division. The design had actually been decreed by the liberal government in 1830, during the civil war, and remained in use until the monarchy was overthrown in 1910.

Portuguese National Flag (1910–present)

Following the revolution of 5 October 1910 and the establishment of the Portuguese Republic, the blue-and-white royal flag was replaced by a vertically divided green-and-red flag. The national coat of arms, surrounded by a white border, is placed over a golden armillary sphere at the division between the colours. The new design was approved by the Provisional Government on 29 November 1910 and formally adopted by decree on 19 June 1911.
