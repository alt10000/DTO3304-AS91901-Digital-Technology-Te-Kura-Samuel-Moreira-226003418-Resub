Historical Flags

Spain’s flag history reflects the union of medieval kingdoms, the dynastic standards of the Habsburgs and Bourbons, the development of military and naval ensigns, and the political changes of the 19th and 20th centuries. Many early designs were royal or military banners rather than national flags in the modern sense.

Royal standard of the Crown of Castile (1230–1406)

The royal banner displayed the arms of Castile: a golden castle on a red field. It represented the Castilian monarchy and was used as a dynastic and military standard after the union of Castile and León under Ferdinand III in 1230.

Royal standard of the Crown of Castile (1406–1500)

The banner continued to feature the golden castle on red, but its heraldic presentation and proportions varied over time. It remained a symbol of the Castilian monarchs before the arms of the Catholic Monarchs and the later Habsburg rulers became prominent.

Royal standard of the Crown of Castile (square shape)

A square version of the Castilian royal banner displayed the castle emblem on a red field. Square banners were practical for ceremonial and military use, although historical depictions do not establish one uniform design or size for every occasion.

Flag of the infantry forces (1475–1506)

Associated with the armies of the Catholic Monarchs, this early military banner is commonly depicted with a red saltire on a light field. Its precise historical form and status as a general flag of Spain are less certain than later standards, so it is best understood as a military banner rather than a formally established national flag.

Cross of Burgundy flag (1506–1701)

A red, jagged-edged diagonal cross (the Cross of Burgundy) on a white field. Introduced into Spain under Philip the Handsome in 1506, it became a widely used military banner and appeared throughout Spain’s European and overseas territories. It was an important symbol of the Spanish monarchy, although it was not the sole flag used by the empire.

Flag of the Spanish Empire (1701–1760)

Under the new Bourbon dynasty of Philip V, white flags bearing the red Cross of Burgundy and the royal coat of arms were used by Spanish forces and in imperial contexts. The arms incorporated the Bourbon-Anjou fleurs-de-lis, marking the change of ruling dynasty.

Flag of the Spanish Empire (1760–1785)

A revised version of the Bourbon-era white flag, retaining the red Cross of Burgundy and royal arms but reflecting changes to the royal coat of arms under Charles III. These white flags were still used before the introduction of the red-and-yellow naval ensign in 1785.

Flag of Spain under Joseph Bonaparte (1808–1813)

A white flag bearing a new royal coat of arms, divided into six sections representing Castile, León, Aragon, Navarre, Granada and the Spanish overseas possessions, with the Bonaparte imperial eagle at its centre. The design was prescribed by Joseph’s decree of 12 July 1808, although its use was limited by the Peninsular War and opposition to his rule.

State flag of Spain (1785–1808; 1813–1873; 1875–1931)

The familiar red-yellow-red horizontal design originated in 1785, when Charles III selected it for the Spanish Navy because it was easier to distinguish at sea than the predominantly white naval flags used by other European powers. The central yellow stripe is twice the width of each red stripe. It was restored after the Napoleonic period and became the standard state flag in the 19th century; the First Republic briefly interrupted its use.

Flag of the First Spanish Republic (1873–1874)

The red-yellow-red flag was retained, but the royal crown was removed from the coat of arms to reflect the abolition of the monarchy. The Republic did not introduce the purple stripe that would later distinguish the Second Republic’s flag.

Flag of the Second Spanish Republic (1931–1939)

A horizontal tricolour of red, yellow and dark purple, with all three stripes of equal width. The purple stripe was intended to represent Castile alongside the traditional red and yellow colours. The state and military versions carried the republican coat of arms, while the civil flag was generally plain.

Flag of Spain (Nationalist faction) (1936–1938)

During the Spanish Civil War, the Nationalist faction initially restored the traditional red-yellow-red bicolour by decree on 29 August 1936. In this early period, different versions and military flags appeared; the familiar flag bearing the Eagle of Saint John was introduced later, in 1938.

Flag of Spain (1938–1945)

The red-yellow-red bicolour was retained, with a coat of arms featuring the Eagle of Saint John placed toward the hoist on the yellow stripe. The emblem drew on the heraldic imagery associated with the Catholic Monarchs and was adopted under the Nationalist government in 1938.

Flag of Spain (1945–1977)

The same basic bicolour and Eagle of Saint John design continued, but the coat of arms was revised and its details formally regulated in 1945. It remained the flag of Spain through the later Franco period and the first stage of the transition following his death in 1975.

Flag of Spain (1977–1981)

The red-yellow-red flag retained the Eagle of Saint John, but the coat of arms was modified again under the regulations of 21 January 1977. This transitional version remained in use until the adoption of the modern coat of arms in 1981.

National flag and ensign of Spain (1981–present)

The current flag consists of three horizontal stripes, red, yellow and red, with the yellow stripe twice as wide as each red stripe. The state flag and naval ensign display the Spanish coat of arms, positioned slightly toward the hoist; the civil flag is plain. The modern coat of arms was established in 1981, and the 1978 Constitution defines the flag’s basic design.

Historical note

The Cross of Burgundy was one of the most recognisable symbols of the Spanish monarchy during the Habsburg era, but Spain did not have one universally standardised national flag throughout this period. The red-and-yellow colours were introduced for naval use in 1785 and gradually became national colours, with their use extended to the Spanish Army in 1843.

Historical clarification

  • The medieval Castilian standards were royal and heraldic banners, not national flags in the modern sense. The square and rectangular versions reflect different banner formats.
  • The Cross of Burgundy was widely used by Spanish forces and across the empire, but it was not the only flag flown in Spanish territories.
  • Although the red-and-yellow design dates to 1785, it was initially a naval ensign, not the general national flag. Its adoption across the Army in 1843 helped establish it as Spain’s national symbol.
  • The First Republic retained the red-and-yellow colours, whereas the Second Republic introduced the red-yellow-purple tricolour.
  • The Eagle of Saint John versions of 1938, 1945 and 1977 were successive revisions of the Franco-era coat of arms, rather than three entirely different flag designs.
