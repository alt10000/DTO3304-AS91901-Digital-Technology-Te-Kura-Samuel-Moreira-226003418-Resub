Sweden

Historical Flags

Swedish flags developed from medieval royal heraldry into the familiar blue flag with a yellow Scandinavian cross. The union with Norway (1814–1905) also influenced several designs, particularly military flags and naval ensigns.

Royal banner of arms of the House of Bjälbo (14th century)

A heraldic banner associated with the House of Bjälbo, also known as the Folkunga dynasty. It is depicted with alternating blue and white horizontal stripes, a golden lion and numerous small hearts. The lion and wavy stripes derive from the dynasty’s heraldic arms, rather than from the later Swedish national flag.

State flag and war ensign (c.1520–mid-17th century)

An early form of the Swedish blue-and-yellow cross flag, generally depicted with a swallowtail or forked fly. Its origins are sometimes associated with Sweden’s struggle against Danish rule around 1520, but the precise date of its introduction is uncertain. The earliest reliable depictions of a blue flag with a yellow cross date from the 16th century; a royal warrant in 1569 specified the yellow cross for Swedish battle standards.

State flag and war ensign (mid-17th century–1815)

The blue flag with a yellow Scandinavian cross continued in use, with a three-tailed swallowtail form serving as the royal and naval ensign. A royal ordinance of 1663 formally distinguished this flag from the square-cut flags permitted to merchant vessels. The design became the basis of Sweden’s later military ensign.

State flag and military ensign (1815–1844)

Following the establishment of the union between Sweden and Norway, the Swedish military flag gained a red canton in the upper hoist containing a white diagonal cross. The rest of the flag retained the blue field, yellow Nordic cross and swallowtail shape. This was a common union war ensign used by the navies and armed forces of both kingdoms, while Norway retained the right to use its own merchant flag.

Naval jack and diplomatic flag (1844–1905)

The union badge, sometimes nicknamed the herring salad, combined the colours of Sweden and Norway in a quartered cross design. It was used independently as the common naval jack and flown at joint Swedish-Norwegian diplomatic and consular missions. It was also incorporated into the national and military flags of both kingdoms.

National flag and civil ensign (1844–1905)

Sweden’s blue flag with a yellow Scandinavian cross was modified by adding the red-and-white union badge in the upper hoist canton. Unlike the military ensign, the civil flag was rectangular rather than swallow-tailed. The badge represented the shared monarchy and the union with Norway; it was removed from Sweden’s civil flag after the union dissolved in 1905.

National flag and civil ensign (1905–1906)

Following the dissolution of the union with Norway in 1905, the union badge was removed, restoring the plain blue field and yellow cross. This was the immediate predecessor of the present Swedish flag. The new arrangement was formally regulated by the Flag Act of 22 June 1906, which also established standard proportions and colour specifications.

Civil and state flag and ensign (1906–present)

Sweden’s flag uses the blue and gold of its national arms, which show three gold crowns on blue, with a Nordic cross copied from Denmark’s Dannebrog. The 1906 Flag Act set its form after the union with Norway ended, and the current flag law took effect on 1 January 1983.

Historical note

The Swedish flag’s blue and yellow colours are traditionally associated with the Swedish coat of arms. The yellow cross was established as a feature of Swedish military banners in the 16th century, while the three-tailed naval flag was formally regulated in 1663. The union with Norway introduced a combined badge in 1844, but that emblem disappeared from Swedish flags following the union’s dissolution in 1905.

Historical clarification

  • The House of Bjälbo banner was a medieval dynastic heraldic standard, not an early version of the modern Swedish national flag.
  • The 1520 date for the blue-and-yellow cross flag is approximate. The earliest reliable surviving depictions are from the 16th century, and the flag’s exact origin remains uncertain.
  • The 1815 flag was a shared military and naval ensign of the Sweden–Norway union; it was not the ordinary merchant flag of both countries.
  • The 1844 union badge served both as an independent naval jack and diplomatic flag and as a canton emblem in the flags of the two kingdoms.
  • The 1905 and 1906 flags were essentially the same design. The first reflected the removal of the union badge, while the 1906 legislation standardised the flag’s proportions and colours.
